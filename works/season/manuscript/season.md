# Part One: Spring 1998

## One

The hallway was empty in the way that only happens early in the morning before the buses came and the building filled with noise and the particular obligation of being seen. Austin had come in early because he'd told himself he would finish the reading for third period before school, which he had not done, and was now walking the hallway with no particular destination because sitting in the library pretending to read seemed worse than simply existing somewhere until the building woke up.

He was walking past the history room when Calloway's voice came through the open door.

"Decker."

Not a question. Austin stopped and looked in.

Mr. Calloway was at his desk with a coffee mug and a folder open in front of him, already in the sweatpants and gray t-shirt he wore every day regardless of weather or occasion or what anyone thought about it. The room smelled like old paper and something Austin had come to associate specifically with this class, which he had not expected to like and did.

"Close the door."

Austin did, and took the chair Mr. Calloway indicated across from the desk — a student chair pulled alongside rather than the confrontational arrangement of a disciplinary meeting. He noted the difference.

"You know what Academic Decathlon is," Calloway said. It was not a question. It rarely was from Mr. Calloway.

"Vaguely."

"Vaguely." Calloway picked up his mug. "Ten subjects. Team of nine. You study all year, competition starts in February. If you win regionals, you go to state in March. We've won the small schools bracket at state more years than not." He paused. "A few years back we were overall state champions and went to nationals."

Austin waited.

"I want you on the team next year."

Outside in the parking lot a car door closed. A few classrooms away, something metallic shifted against a radiator — the building settling into the morning.

"Why," Austin said.

Calloway looked at him for a moment with the expression Austin had catalogued over the past eight months — not unfriendly but not warm, simply the face of a man who was paying attention and did not feel the need to disguise it.

"The person who sits in the third row of my class," Calloway said, "is not the same person I see in the hallway or that I've read about in your transcripts." He let that land. "I'm interested in the one in the third row."

Austin didn't say anything to that.

"The commitment is real," Calloway said. "I'm not going to tell you otherwise. Practices run to six most nights early in the year, then after Thanksgiving we go until nine Monday through Thursday, add Saturday mornings and Sunday afternoons. That's your year from November to March — April if we go to nationals." He set down the mug. "I'm not asking you to decide right now. I'm asking you to take it seriously and come back to me."

"How will you know if I took it seriously?"

"I'll know," Calloway said. His look shut down the flippant reply on the tip of Austin's tongue.

Austin stood. At the door he paused — not entirely sure why, some instinct to extend the conversation before it closed.

"Is this the part where you tell me it'll change my life?"

Calloway had already turned back to his folder. "Go to class, Decker."

## Two

Nolan's mother always made popcorn the old-fashioned way, in a pot on the stove, and left it in a bowl on the counter without fanfare or preamble. This was how things worked at the Farrell house — food appeared when it was needed and nobody performed the act of providing it. Austin had been eating this family's food for nine years.

They were watching *The Blues Brothers* because Nolan owned it on tape and it was the kind of movie that worked at midnight in a way that demanded nothing from you. Jake and Elwood were a hundred and six miles from Chicago, a full tank of gas, half a pack of cigarettes, it was dark, and they were wearing sunglasses. Nolan could quote the whole film and sometimes did, not loudly, just mouthing the words slightly ahead of the actors the way some people did with songs they'd heard too many times. Austin had long stopped finding this strange.

It was nearly one in the morning. Nolan's mother had been in bed for two hours. Somewhere outside, one of the older brothers had come by earlier — Austin had heard his truck in the driveway and then heard it leave — and the house had settled back into its particular quiet, which was different from other houses' quiet in ways Austin had never been able to articulate.

"Can I ask you something," Austin said.

"You're going to anyway."

"That's fair." He moved the popcorn bowl from his lap to the cushion between them. On screen Jake Blues was attempting to explain himself to the penguin. "Did Mrs. Voss talk to you about decathlon?"

Nolan was quiet for a beat longer than he would have been if it were unremarkable information.

"Yeah," he said.

"When."

"Couple weeks ago."

Austin looked at him. "And you didn't say anything."

"You didn't either."

"I've known for three days."

"I've known for three weeks." Nolan reached over for a handful of popcorn without looking away from the screen. "I needed to think about it first."

"Have you?"

"Some." He chewed. "Have you?"

"Some," Austin said.

They watched the movie for a while. The Bluesmobile was shedding itself across a parking lot. Austin had seen this enough times that he could hold it in his peripheral vision while actually thinking about something else, which was what he was doing.

"What did she say to you," he said. "Mrs. Voss."

"That she wanted me on the team. That she'd seen my work in her class and thought I'd be good at the art portion." Nolan paused. "That it was a serious commitment and to think it over."

"Same thing Calloway told me."

"That's probably their whole speech."

"Probably," Austin said. "Except Calloway's version has about forty percent less warmth."

Nolan almost smiled at this. He had a way of almost smiling that was more satisfying than most people's actual smiles. "How'd he even know to ask you?"

"He said the person in his class is different from the person in the hallway." Austin stopped. He hadn't meant to say it that directly.

Nolan was quiet for a moment. He didn't react to this the way most people would have — didn't make it into something, didn't push on it. Just received it.

"That's interesting," he said.

"I know."

"Is he right?"

Austin looked at the television. "Probably," he said.

They let that sit. On screen the mall chase had started. Nolan watched it with his feet on the coffee table, which his mother tolerated but did not endorse, and finished the handful of popcorn, and didn't say anything else about what Austin had said, which was part of why Austin had said it in the first place.

"The thing is," Austin said, eventually, "I've been trying to figure out why I don't want to do it."

"Because of the time."

"That's the answer I keep giving myself. But I give up time for stuff. I drove forty minutes to Brownwood twice last month to play chess with a couple of old teachers who drink bad coffee and take three minutes per move."

"Right."

"So it's not really the time."

"No," Nolan agreed. "Probably not."

"I think it's more like —" Austin searched for it. "It's a different thing. The chess thing is mine. Nobody at school knows about it. It's just a thing I do." He paused. "This would be a school thing. In the building. With people who know me."

Nolan considered this seriously, the way he considered most things. "You'd have to actually try," he said. Not an accusation. Just the observation.

"Yeah."

"In front of people."

"Yeah."

"That's the thing, then."

"That's the thing," Austin agreed.

A while passed. The movie wound toward its conclusion, cars flying off bridges, a hundred and six miles accomplished.

"What would you do it for," Austin said. "If you did it."

Nolan thought about this for longer than Austin expected him to. "I think I'd want to see if I could," he said finally. "I don't know if I'm actually smart or if I just seem that way in the classes I'm in." He looked at the ceiling briefly. "This would be a way to find out."

Austin hadn't expected that. He thought about it.

"That's a better reason than mine," he said.

"What's yours."

Austin was quiet for a moment. "I don't know yet," he said, which was honest.

Nolan nodded slowly and didn't push further. This was one of the things about him that Austin had always valued and never said out loud.

The movie ended. Nolan hit rewind on the remote and stood and stretched and said he was going to bed. Austin said he'd let himself out in the morning, the way he always did, and Nolan went down the hall and Austin pulled the blanket off the back of the couch — the same blanket that had been there since before Austin could remember, washed into a kind of permanent softness — and lay back and looked at the ceiling while the tape rewound in the dark.

Outside, the rural dark was total. No streetlights out this far, just the fields and whatever was beyond them. He'd driven out here on these Saturday nights since he was old enough to borrow his father's car, and before that had been dropped off, and the drive back in the morning was always the same — the fields doing whatever fields did at that time of year, and Hartwell resolving out of the distance like a place that had always been there and would be there again.

He wasn't sure what he'd expected Nolan to say. He wasn't sure what he'd expected himself to say either. But the thing was settled now in the way things got settled between them — not decided so much as recognized, the way you recognized something that had already been true before you put words to it.

He went to sleep.
