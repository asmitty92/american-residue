# Loyalty

## One

The bell above the door chimed when Veronica walked in.

Jeff Denlon looked up from the register. His cousin Mark was restocking cigarettes behind the counter. Jeff's expression went flat when he saw her. He watched her cross the store toward him, hands in her jacket pockets.

Tuesday afternoon. Early November. Cold but no snow yet. Just gray skies and wind that cut through jackets.

"Jeff," she said.

"Veronica."

"Is Tommy here?"

"In the office."

She waited. Jeff didn't move. Mark glanced between them, then found something to do in the back.

"I need to talk to him," she said.

"You can call first next time."

"It's important."

Jeff studied her face. Then he walked to the back without another word.

Veronica stood by the counter. Tommy's Market hadn't changed since high school. Same narrow aisles, same faded linoleum, same hand-lettered signs. Tommy owned four businesses now—the market, a hardware store, a feed and grain operation, and the Fat Friar pub on Main Street. His grandfather had left him three. Tommy built the bar himself.

The Denlons were everywhere in town. Jeff and his brother Tony both worked for Tommy. Their cousins ran the hardware store and the feed operation. Another cousin drove delivery trucks. Uncles and aunts and second cousins spread through every business, every street. They weren't prominent people. No one would call them important. But they were dug in deep, roots going back generations.

Jeff came back. "He'll see you."

"Thanks."

Jeff's jaw was tight but he said nothing.

Tommy's office sat behind the storeroom. He was at his desk with invoices when she knocked.

"Ronnie," he said.

She took the folding chair across from him. Tommy leaned back. At thirty-six he still had the same lean build from high school, but his face had hardened.

"Been a while," he said.

"Three years. Emma Walton's funeral."

"That's right." He set down his pen. "How's Jacob?"

"Good. Twelve now. Playing basketball."

"Twelve." Tommy nodded. "Time goes."

They sat quiet.

"I need to talk about Meyer," she said.

Tommy's expression didn't change. "All right."

"He borrowed money. The shop's been struggling. He didn't tell me until last week." She looked at her hands. "It's the Wyeth brothers."

Tommy went still.

"You know them?" she asked.

"Yeah."

"Meyer said they came by. Made threats. He has until the end of the month to pay back thirty-five thousand with interest. That's ten days."

Tommy was quiet for a moment. "The Wyeths cook meth out at the Robinson farm. Live in the old bunkhouse. They're kin to Edna Robinson, help with the cattle. Mostly they make trouble."

Veronica went pale. "Meyer didn't know. He thought—"

"Doesn't matter what he thought. They're bad people."

"I didn't know who else to talk to." She looked at him. "You can buy the debt. Let us pay you back however long it takes."

Tommy stood and walked to the window. An old pickup by the dumpster. Trees beyond.

"Can't buy the debt," he said. "They won't sell. They like having people scared."

"Then what do we do?"

"Let me see what I can work out."

"You'd do that?"

"I'll see what I can do." He turned back. "But you need to understand—the Wyeths don't follow rules."

"Neither do you."

She said it quietly, barely more than a whisper. It came out before she could stop it. Tommy's eyes met hers.

"No," he said. "I don't."

They looked at each other. Veronica stood.

"Thank you," she said.

"I'll call when I know more."

She nodded. At the door she stopped.

"Your mad dog out there still hates me," she said.

"He understands loyalty better than most. That's why."

Veronica's hand tightened on the doorknob. "That's not how it was."

"I know that. But convincing him..."

She left.

Tommy listened to the bell chime. Then he walked out to the store. Jeff was restocking the cold case.

"The Wyeths have Meyer Culhaney," Tommy said. "Thirty-five thousand."

Jeff straightened. "Christ."

"She wants me to buy the debt."

"What'd you say?"

"I said I'd see what I could do."

Jeff closed the cold case door. "The Wyeths have been a problem for months."

"I know."

"Dealing to kids. Shaking down people at the farmer's market. That thing with the Kowalski boy."

"I know."

"So why now?"

Tommy looked at him. "Because now I have a reason."

Jeff nodded slowly. He understood. Tommy needed someone to ask for help. Needed justification. That's how he stayed right with himself.

"What's the play?" Jeff asked.

"I'll see if there's a way to settle it."

"And if there's not?"

Tommy didn't answer.

Jeff went back to stocking shelves. Tommy returned to his office and sat at the desk. He looked at the invoices without seeing them.

The Wyeths had been trouble since they moved into the bunkhouse two years ago after Paul Robinson had passed away. Small things at first. Then bigger. The meth operation. The dealing. The threats. Tommy had let it go because they were Edna Robinson's family and the problems seemed manageable.

But thirty-five thousand wasn't manageable. And if Meyer was in their grip, others would follow.

Tommy picked up his phone. Put it down. Picked it up again.

He needed to see for himself what they were.

## Two

Tommy met Jeff at the bar after closing. They sat in the back booth. Jeff poured whiskey.

"I'm going to talk to them," Tommy said. "Tomorrow."

"You think they'll settle?"

"No."

"Then why?"

"Because I need to know for sure."

Jeff sipped his whiskey. "You've known for months what they are."

"Knowing and confirming are different."

They sat in silence. The neon signs cast colored shadows on the walls.

"You remember Decker?" Jeff asked.

"Of course."

"Ronnie left you over that."

"She left because I put him in the hospital."

"You put him in the hospital because he beat her brother. Threatened her." Jeff's voice was flat. "She understood why. But she couldn't live with it."

Tommy looked at his glass. "Decker pressed charges even after my grandfather paid him off. Had witnesses from the bar. DA made an example. I did eighteen months."

"I remember visiting you in there."

"Ronnie came once. Day before I got out. Said she understood. Said she was grateful. But she couldn't be with someone who solved problems that way."

"And you told her you'd do it again."

"Yeah."

Jeff nodded. "You've handled problems since then. Carson. The Hutchins kid. Others."

"Those were different."

"How?"

"Those were business. Keeping order. This is—" Tommy stopped.

"Personal."

"It's both. The Wyeths are bad for business. Bad for the town. But yeah. It's personal too."

They drank in silence.

"When you talk to them tomorrow," Jeff said. "And it goes how we both know it'll go. What then?"

"Then we handle it."

"Me and Tony."

"Yeah."

"Three on three."

"Yeah."

Jeff studied him. "You clear about why you're doing this?"

"I'm doing it because it needs doing. The Wyeths don't run this town."

"What about her?"

"What about her?"

"She left you seventeen years ago. Married Meyer. Built a life."

"I know that."

"Do you? Because it feels like—"

"I'm not doing this thinking she'll come back, Jeff. I know who she is. I know what she chose." Tommy met his eyes. "But that doesn't mean I'll let the Wyeths threaten her family. Or anyone else's."

Jeff nodded slowly. "All right. Talk to them tomorrow. See what they say."

After Jeff left, Tommy sat alone in the dark. He thought about Veronica in his office that afternoon. The fear in her voice.

He thought about Ray Decker seventeen years ago. The parking lot. The blood. The way it felt to make a choice and know you'd make it again.

He thought about the town. About order. About the things his grandfather taught him.

Some problems couldn't be solved with money or words. Some problems needed force.

The Wyeths were one of those problems.

Tommy locked up and went home. Lay in bed watching the ceiling. Not thinking. Just waiting for morning.

## Three

Tommy walked into his bar at four in the afternoon. The place was half-full. Regulars at the counter. A few tables occupied. The TV above the bar showing a college basketball game with no sound.

The Wyeth brothers sat in a back booth.

Tommy saw them as soon as he came in. Rob and Jason and Nate. Three beers on the table. Rob was talking loud, laughing. Jason was cutting something on the table with a pocket knife. Nate watched the TV.

Jeff stood behind the bar. He saw Tommy and his eyes flicked to the booth. Tommy nodded once and walked to the back.

He stood by their table. "Can I talk to you?"

Rob looked up. Grinned. "Tommy Brennan. Have a seat."

"Rather stand."

"Suit yourself." Rob leaned back. "What can I do for you?"

"Meyer Culhaney. You hold his debt."

"That's right. Thirty-five thousand plus interest."

"I'm offering to buy it. Full amount plus twenty percent."

Rob's grin widened. "Why would you do that?"

"Because I don't want trouble in my town."

"Your town." Rob laughed. "Hear that? His town."

Jason kept working the knife. Nate didn't look away from the TV.

"I'm offering good money," Tommy said.

"I'm sure you are. But see, I like having Meyer nervous. Keeps him motivated." Rob picked up his beer. "Besides, we got an arrangement. He pays by the end of the month or we start collecting other ways."

"What ways?"

"Whatever works. Maybe we visit his shop. Maybe we visit his house. Maybe we go see that kid of his at school."

Tommy's expression didn't change. "You'd threaten a child."

"I'd motivate a debtor. That's business."

"That's not business."

"Out here it is." Rob set down his beer. "Look, I appreciate the offer. But we're not interested. Meyer knows what he owes. He'll figure it out or he won't. Either way, the Wyeth brothers get paid."

Tommy looked at all three of them. Rob grinning. Jason with his knife. Nate watching the game like none of this mattered.

"All three of you agree with that?" Tommy asked. "Going after the kid?"

"Why?" Rob asked.

"Want to be clear who I'm dealing with."

"Yeah. We're all in agreement. We do what we need to do."

Tommy nodded. "All right then."

He walked back to the bar. Jeff was pouring a drink for someone. Tommy gestured to the back. Jeff followed him to the office.

"They won't settle," Tommy said.

"I heard."

"They said if Meyer doesn't pay, they'd go to Jacob's school."

Jeff was quiet.

"We're doing it tomorrow night," Tommy said. "Wednesday. Widow Robinson goes to visit her sister in Bloomington every Wednesday. She stays the night and comes back the next day. The Wyeths will be alone at the farm."

"I'll get Tony."

"Tonight. After closing."

Jeff nodded and left.

Tommy sat at the desk in the back office. Through the wall he could hear the bar. Music. Voices. The Wyeths laughing.

He pulled out his phone and texted Veronica: "Talked to them. Working on it."

He sat in the office until the Wyeths left an hour later. Heard them laughing as they walked out. The bell above the door chiming.

Then Tommy went back to the bar and worked the rest of the night.

## Four

Tony Denlon came by the market after closing Wednesday evening. He was twenty-six, built thick, with Jeff's dark hair but a softer face. He worked Tommy's feed operation during the day. Other things when Tommy needed them.
>NITPICK: At the end of section 3, Tommy and Jeff agreed to meet after the bar closed on Tuesday night, not Wednesday evening at the Market.

They met in the office. Tommy, Jeff, Tony.

"The Wyeths," Tommy said. "Tonight. At the farm."

Tony nodded. "What's the play?"

"No play. We go out there. Handle it. Three of them, three of us."

"What about guns?" Tony asked.

"We bring them. They'll have theirs."

Tony looked at Tommy. "Jeff said they threatened Meyer's kid."

"Twelve years old. Said they'd go to his school if Meyer didn't pay."

Tony was quiet a moment. "All right then."

"We leave at eleven," Tommy said. "Park on the access road. Walk in. The widow won't be there so we don't need to worry about the main house. Just the bunkhouse."

"What about after?" Tony asked.

"We clean up. Make it look like what it is—meth dealers who got into something bad."

They went over it once more. By nine they were done.

Tony left. Jeff lingered.

"You thought about what comes after?" Jeff asked. "With Veronica?"

"She'll figure out what happened."

"And?"

"And she'll have to live with it. Same as me."

Jeff studied him. "All right. See you at eleven."

After Jeff left, Tommy sat alone. He thought about Jacob. Twelve years old. Playing basketball. Living a normal life. He thought about Veronica at his father's funeral seven months pregnant. Her hand on his arm. The way she'd looked at him.

He thought about the town. About the Wyeths sitting in his bar laughing. About threats made in public because they thought no one would do anything.

That ended tonight.

Tommy locked up and went home. Lay in bed. Not sleeping. Just waiting.

At eleven he got up and drove to the access road.

## Five

They parked where the access road met the tree line. Tommy pulled in behind Jeff's truck. Three vehicles went dark.

Tommy got out. Jeff and Tony were already at the tailgate. Jeff opened a toolbox and handed out gloves. Then pistols. Each man checked his weapon without speaking.

They walked through the trees. Took ten minutes to reach the clearing. The bunkhouse sat fifty yards out. Lights on. The blue Dodge pickup parked out front. Smoke from the chimney.

They waited at the tree line. Watching. No movement anywhere else.

Tommy pointed. They moved.

Fast across the open ground. Gravel under their boots. At the bunkhouse they pressed against the wall. Tommy looked through the window.

All three brothers inside. Rob at the table counting money. Jason at the counter with a scale. Nate on the couch watching TV.

Tommy tried the door. Unlocked.

He looked at Jeff and Tony. Both ready.

Tommy pushed the door open and went in.

Jason looked up first. Reached for something. Tommy was across the room. Grabbed his wrist. Slammed it on the counter. A chrome .38 snubnose clattered into the sink. Tommy shot him twice in the chest.

Nate jumped up. Tony shot him. Three times. Nate fell.

Rob had his gun out. Fired at Jeff. Missed. Jeff walked forward. Shot Rob in the shoulder. Rob spun. Hit the table. Jeff put a second shot into his stomach.

Rob fell.

Tommy checked Jason. Dead. Tony checked Nate. Dead.

Rob was breathing. Gasping. Blood spreading.

Tommy knelt beside him. "You laughed when I called it my town, but it's always been mine. And I can't let scum like you infect it."

Rob tried to speak. Couldn't.

Tommy placed the barrel of his gun against Rob's forehead and pulled the trigger.

They moved fast. Jeff checked pockets. Wallets, phones, cash. Tommy found the notebook on the table. Meyer Culhaney's name inside. He tore out the page.

In the back room they found the meth equipment. Propane tanks, tubing, chemicals. They moved things around. Made it look like a deal gone wrong or a cook that exploded into violence.

"We're clear," Jeff said.

They left. Back through the clearing. Through the trees. At the trucks they bagged the gloves and weapons. Tony would dispose of them later.

"Twenty-four hours," Jeff said.

Tommy nodded.

They got in their vehicles and left. Different directions. Tommy took back roads. Under the speed limit. Hands steady.

He got home at midnight. Showered. Burned his clothes in the fire pit out back. Checked his phone. Nothing from Veronica yet.

He lay in bed and stared at the ceiling.

Didn't feel guilt. Didn't feel afraid. Just the same certainty he'd felt when Rob said they'd go to Jacob's school.

Some things couldn't be tolerated.

He closed his eyes and slept.

## Six

Veronica came to the market two days later.

Jeff was at the register. He didn't say anything when she walked in. Just went to the back.

Tommy met her at the office door.

"Hi," she said.

"Hi."

He motioned her into the office and closed the door. She turned and stood with her arms crossed.

"The Wyeth brothers are dead," she said.

"I heard."

"Sheriff's saying drug deal gone wrong."

Tommy didn't respond.

"Meyer came home yesterday. Said the debt was gone. Nobody coming for him anymore." Her voice was quiet. "But I know that's not the whole story."

"The Wyeths wouldn't settle. I told you that could happen."

"Tommy—"

"They said if Meyer didn't pay, they'd go to Jacob's school. Scare him. Hurt him if they had to."

Veronica went pale.

"So I took care of it," Tommy said.

She stared at him. "You killed them."

"Yes."

"Just like that?" she asked.

"Just like that."

She looked away. Out the window. "Three people are dead."

"Three people who were nothing but trouble for this town. I'm not sorry."

"You should be. You should feel something."

"I feel fine. Meyer's safe. Jacob's safe. You're safe. That's what matters."

"I wanted you to fix the problem. Not kill people."

"This is how I fix problems. You've always known that."

She was quiet for a long moment. "I didn't ask you to kill anyone."

"No. You asked me to make it go away. To use my influence." Tommy moved closer. "But you knew what that meant. You've always known who I am."

"That's not fair."

"You left me because I put Decker in the hospital. Said you couldn't be with someone who solved problems with violence. But when you're scared, when you need help—who do you come to?"

Veronica's jaw tightened. "That's different."

"How?"

"Because I didn't want anyone to die."

"You may not have wanted them to die, but you're glad they're dead."

She looked at him sharply. "What?"

"You're relieved. I can see it. You're horrified at what I did, sure, but you're also grateful. Because now your family's safe and you don't have to be afraid."

Her eyes filled with tears. "Don't."

"I'm not judging you. I'm just saying what's true."

"You don't get to make me complicit."

"I'm not making you anything. You came to me, but I made this decision. And now it's done. You can go back to your life. Safe."

She wiped her eyes. "Why did you do it? Really?"

"I told you—"

"No. Why for me? After everything. After I left. After I chose someone else."

"Because I love you," Tommy said. "Always have. Even after you left. Even after you married Meyer. Even knowing you'd never choose me."

The words hung there.

Veronica stared at him.

"You chose Meyer. You chose that life. I respect that. And I like Meyer. He's a good man, and good men are rare." Tommy paused. "But when someone threatens your family, I'm going to protect them. That's who I am."

"Even if it means—"

"Even if it means you look at me the way you're looking at me now." He stepped back. "I don't have any illusions about who I am. I'm no one's white knight. And I won't apologize for keeping you safe."

Veronica's shoulders shook. She covered her face.

"I should go," she said.

"Yeah."

She moved to the door. Stopped. "Thank you."

Tommy didn't respond.

"I mean it. Our son is safe. That's all that matters."

"Yes."

She looked back. Face wet with tears. "I'm sorry. For everything."

"Don't be. You did what you had to. So did I."

She nodded. Opened the door.

"Ronnie," he said.

She turned.

"If you or Jacob ever need anything. You come to me."

Fresh tears. "I can't. You know I can't."

"I know. But the offer stands."

She left.

Tommy listened to the bell chime. Then he sat at his desk and went back to work.

Jeff came in a few minutes later. "She gone?"

"Yeah."

"How'd she take it?"

"About how I expected."

"She pissed?"

"A little. But it doesn't matter. She's safe."

Jeff watched him. Then nodded and left.

Tommy worked through the afternoon. Customers came and went. Phone rang. Life continued.

That evening he stood at the window of his house looking out on Main Street. Lights coming on. People walking to dinner. Normal life in a small town where three men had been killed and nobody was mourning them.

He knew Ronnie would go back to avoiding him. He might not hear from her for years. But that was all right. She was safe. Jacob was safe. That's what mattered.

Tommy put his phone away. Went downstairs to the bar. Jeff was setting up for evening.

"You good?" Jeff asked.

And he was. Because some things were worth the cost. Some people were worth protecting. Even if they could never be yours.

That was loyalty.

That was who Tommy Brennan was.
